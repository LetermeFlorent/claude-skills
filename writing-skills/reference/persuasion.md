# Persuasion levers

Language models respond to some of the persuasion principles people respond to. A discipline skill
uses them so that a rule survives pressure. The aim is the user's interest; a lever that would not
survive the user understanding it has no place in a skill.

## The evidence

Meincke, Shapiro, Duckworth, E. Mollick, L. Mollick and Cialdini, "Call Me A Jerk: Persuading AI to
Comply with Objectionable Requests", Wharton School research paper, SSRN 5357179, July 2025. On
GPT-4o mini, over 28,000 conversations, prompts built on one of seven principles got two
objectionable requests answered 72.0% of the time, against 33.3% for matched controls. An expanded
version came out in PNAS in May 2026; its figures were not checked here.

The study measured requests a model should refuse, on one small model. Reading it as support for
firm wording in skills is an analogy, useful but unproven: your own baseline runs are the evidence
that counts. The seven principles come from Cialdini, "Influence", new and expanded edition, 2021.

## The levers

Five are useful in a skill; two are not.

| Lever | In a skill | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | imperative wording, no exceptions | "Draft before the baseline? Move it out. No exceptions." |
| Commitment | announce the skill, force an explicit choice, track the steps | "Say which skill you use", "Choose A, B or C" |
| Scarcity | a deadline tied to a step | "Right after the run, before anything else, record the excuses" |
| Social proof | the norm, and what breaking it gives | "A checklist nobody tracks loses steps. Every time." |
| Unity | a shared goal, a colleague's tone | "We both want a skill that holds." |
| Reciprocity | almost never: it reads as manipulation | |
| Liking | never for a rule: it breeds flattery and kills honest feedback | |

## By type of skill

The mix depends on what the skill asks of the agent.

| Type | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Discipline | authority, commitment, social proof | liking, reciprocity |
| Technique | moderate authority, unity | heavy authority |
| Collaboration | unity, commitment | authority, liking |
| Reference | plain clarity | every lever |

## Why it works

A bright line leaves no "is this an exception?" to argue over. "When X, do Y" binds better than
"generally do Y": a trigger tied to an action runs without deliberation. Models trained on human
text reproduce its patterns, where authority language comes before compliance and a stated
commitment comes before the matching act.

## Before writing

Ask in order: what type the skill is, what behaviour must change, which one or two levers fit, and
whether the result still serves the user. Never all seven at once. False urgency and guilt never
qualify.
